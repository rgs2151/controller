Abstract

Brains and large language models need not be considered equivalent systems to reveal an interpretable commonality: both exhibit high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics whose behavior can only be modeled imperfectly. This creates a shared challenge whenever we seek to intervene in their dynamics. A controller may either rely on a model identified around a particular operating regime and risk failure as the system moves away from that regime, or explicitly account for uncertainty and a changing internal state. Neurostimulation has confronted an analogous problem for decades. The limitations of fixed, open-loop stimulation have motivated closed-loop and state-dependent approaches that condition intervention on the ongoing state of the neural system. Inspired by this principle, we formulate LLM steering as a feedback-control problem in which the network’s internal state, rather than a fixed intervention alone, determines how the system is perturbed.

We further account for the inevitable mismatch between tractable dynamical models and the nonlinear transformer they approximate by adopting a robust control approach. As one principled realization of this framework, we develop an \(H_\infty\)-based steering controller that explicitly limits sensitivity to disturbances and linearization mismatches. Across multiple steering benchmarks, the resulting controller improves performance over existing steering baselines, with particularly pronounced gains under out-of-distribution conditions and varying length complexities—regimes in which the assumptions underlying locally identified dynamics are increasingly challenged. Beyond controller performance, we introduce robust steerability, \(S_{\mathrm{rob}}\), a pre-deployment measure of tolerance to dynamics mismatch; across model–task pairs, it exhibits task-specific structure and varies systematically with model scale. Beyond robust LLM steering, our measure asks not merely whether control is possible, but how much uncertainty it can survive—a principle that may span artificial and biological neural systems.

A successful perturbation is not the same as reliable control.

  1. First, the effect of stimulation depends on the state in which it is delivered: across invasive and non-invasive paradigms, state alters the magnitude, direction and behavioral consequence of otherwise identical stimulation.

  2. At deployment, shifts in prompt, context, or activation trajectory alter the network’s local Jacobians and therefore how the same intervention propagates. Distribution shift thus changes not only the state being controlled but also the dynamics of control itself.

  3. Language models and biological circuits are not equivalent systems, but both are high-dimensional, nonlinear and imperfectly modeled, and intervention in both inherits the same reliability problem.

Can a language model’s internal dynamics tell us, before deployment, whether a representation will remain reliably steerable as those dynamics change?

Robust activation steering

We treat transformer depth as a finite control horizon, hidden representations as states, and activation interventions as control inputs. Given a target behavior, desired and undesired calibration activations define reduced behavior coordinates. Projected Jacobians estimate local dynamics, while empirical residuals identify the directions and scales of model mismatch:

\[\xkp = \Ak\xk + \Bk\uk + \Dk\wk.\]

Here, \(\Ak\) describes nominal layer dynamics, \(\Bk\uk\) the activation intervention, and \(\Dk\wk\) the structured mismatch. Rather than assuming that mismatch is negligible or isotropic, we design the controller against its amplification through the network.

For a candidate attenuation \(\gamma\), robust feedback solves a finite-horizon dynamic game subject to the local dynamics above:

\[\min_{\pi}\max_{w_{1:L}}\;x_{L+1}^{\top}Q_f x_{L+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(\lVert\zk\rVert_2^2-\gamma^2\lVert\wk\rVert_2^2\right).\]

The controller minimizes steering error and intervention cost; the adversary acts through the empirical mismatch channels. The regulated output \(\zk\) combines target-tracking error, intervention effort, and optional off-target representation change. A backward \(H_\infty\) recursion and attenuation search produce the layer-wise policy \(\uk=-\steervar{K_k^\star}\xk.\)

Feedback makes the intervention depend on the current activation state; robustness accounts for the fact that the local model is imperfect. Model weights remain unchanged, and dynamics estimation and controller synthesis are performed offline.

Robust steerability

Intuitively, it characterizes how much deviation from the identified dynamics can be tolerated before the same model is no longer sufficient for reliable intervention.

\[\Srob = \frac{1}{\gammastar}, \qquad \gammastar = \inf_{\steervar{K}} \lVert T_{w\to z}(\steervar{K})\rVert_\infty.\]

A larger \(\Srob\) therefore corresponds to a regime in which steering is less sensitive to modeling error and uncertainty.
